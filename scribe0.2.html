1. Introduction: The Medical Scribe Role

The medical scribe’s primary role is to record detailed information from a patient’s clinic visit into the electronic chart. This allows the healthcare provider to focus on patients and save hours of charting. Scribes are responsible for documenting interviews, physical exams, procedures, clinical progress, and transcribing ancillary test results.

💡 The Golden Rules of Scribing 1. If the doctor discusses it, document it. (Even if it seems extraneous).
2. If it isn't documented, it didn't happen. (Assumptions are dangerous).

Scribe Prohibitions

🚫 Scribes are PROHIBITED from:
  • Interviewing patients or family members independently
  • Examining patients or independently making medical decisions
  • Assisting with medical procedures, specimen collection, or IV placement
  • Any physical interaction with a patient beyond a handshake
  • Logging into the EHR as a physician or signing medical notes
  • Phoning in orders, prescriptions, or serving as a medical translator

HIPAA and Patient Privacy

HIPAA protects Protected Health Information (PHI). Any data that could identify a patient is protected. Scribes must adhere to the "minimum necessary" rule and never access charts out of curiosity (e.g., famous patients, neighbors, or family members).

⚠️ Social Media Warning Social media is the single riskiest area for HIPAA breaches. Never post ANYTHING work-related, even if you omit the patient's name. "I can't say who it was, but the manager at the gas station..." is still a breach.

2. Medical Terminology & Anatomy

Medical terminology is built on Greek and Latin roots. Understanding these allows for concise and accurate documentation.

Anatomical Directions

  • Superior / Inferior: Above / Below (e.g., chest is superior to abdomen).
  • Proximal / Distal: Closer to origin / Farther from origin (used for extremities).
  • Medial / Lateral: Closer to midline / Farther from midline.
  • Anterior / Posterior: Front of body / Back of body.
  • Volar / Plantar: Palms of hands / Soles of feet.

3. The Subjective Sections

The subjective section is based on information the patient tells the provider. It includes the Chief Complaint (CC), History of Present Illness (HPI), and Review of Systems (ROS).

The HPI and OPQRST

The HPI is a chronological narrative of the patient's story. Use the OPQRST mnemonic to clarify symptoms:

LetterMeaningQuestion to Ask
OnsetWhen it started"When did the pain begin?"
ProvocationWhat makes it worse"Does movement or eating make it worse?"
QualityWhat it feels like"Is it sharp, dull, burning, or pressure?"
Region/RadiationLocation & spread"Where is it? Does it move anywhere?"
SeverityIntensity"On a scale of 0-10, how bad is it?"
TimingFrequency/Duration"Is it constant or does it come and go?"

Review of Systems (ROS)

The ROS is a checklist of symptoms across body systems not covered in the HPI. It is crucial for billing and comprehensive care.

4. The Physical Exam

The physical exam represents the provider's objective findings. It is documented in a head-to-toe manner using medical terminology. Symptoms do not belong here unless elicited by a physical maneuver.

📝 Documentation Tip Avoid writing just "Normal". Instead, specify what was examined: "TMs clear bilaterally. External canals unremarkable." rather than "Ears: Normal."

Common Exam Terminology

  • HEENT: Normocephalic, atraumatic (NC/AT). Pupils equal, round, reactive to light (PERRL). Extraocular movements intact (EOMI).
  • Cardiovascular: Regular rate and rhythm (RRR). No murmurs, gallops, or rubs.
  • Respiratory: Clear to auscultation (CTA) bilaterally. Good air movement.
  • Abdomen: Soft, non-tender, non-distended (NTND). Normoactive bowel sounds (NABS).
  • Neurological: Alert and oriented x3 (A&Ox3). Cranial nerves II-XII grossly intact.

5. Objective Sections: Clinical Course & A&P

The Clinical Course outlines the timeline of the visit (labs, imaging, therapies). The Assessment & Plan (A&P) is the culmination of the note, detailing the diagnoses and the treatment plan.

Clinical Decision Making (CDM / MDM)

The MDM explains the physician's thought process. It synthesizes the history, exam, and workup to justify the diagnoses and plan. It must address why dangerous conditions were ruled out.

ICD-10 Diagnoses

Diagnoses should be as specific as possible, including laterality, acuity (acute/chronic), and severity. E.g., "Acute on chronic systolic congestive heart failure" rather than just "Heart failure".

6. Primary Care vs. Emergency Department

🏥 Primary Care Clinic
  • Focus on preventative care, chronic disease management, and healthcare maintenance.
  • Visits are scheduled; flow is predictable.
  • Common encounters: Annual physicals, Well-Child Checks, DM/HTN management.
🚑 Emergency Department
  • Focus on ruling out life-threatening conditions ("Rule-Out" methodology).
  • Unpredictable, high-acuity, fast-paced environment.
  • Common encounters: Chest pain, Abdominal pain, Trauma, AMS.

ED "Rule-Out" Methodology

For a complaint like Chest Pain, the ED physician must first rule out Myocardial Infarction (MI) and Pulmonary Embolism (PE) before considering benign causes like GERD or musculoskeletal strain. The MDM must explicitly document this rationale.

7. Billing, E/M Levels, & Quality Measures

Accurate documentation ensures fair reimbursement. Evaluation and Management (E/M) levels (1-5) determine how much payers reimburse.

2021 Outpatient E/M Changes

📊 The Shift from "Bean Counting" Prior to 2021, billing relied on counting elements in the HPI, ROS, and Physical Exam. The 2021 guidelines shifted the focus to Medical Decision Making (MDM) and Total Time spent on the encounter.

MDM Complexity Levels

LevelComplexityExamples
1StraightforwardMinor issue, minimal risk (e.g., simple rash)
2LowStable chronic illness, OTC meds
3ModerateAcute illness with systemic symptoms, new problem with uncertain prognosis
4HighSevere exacerbation, chronic illness with severe progression, decision for admission

Quality Measures (MIPS)

Providers are financially incentivized (or penalized) based on quality reporting. Examples include: Screening for depression (PHQ-9), controlling high blood pressure, appropriate antibiotic use for URIs, and fall risk assessments in the elderly.

8. The Medical Note Big Picture

A thorough note accurately captures today's visit in a standardized fashion. It is not just for the provider today; it is a legal document that will be read by future physicians, auditors, insurance companies, and the patients themselves via patient portals.

🌟 Final Golden Rules for the Master Scribe
  1. If the physician discusses it or does it, document it.
  2. If it isn't documented, it didn't happen.
  3. Avoid regrets. Write with the presumption that a patient or a lawyer may read it.
  4. Interpretation is key. Don't just list lab results; document that the provider reviewed and interpreted them.
